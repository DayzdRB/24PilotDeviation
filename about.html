Putin Princess
Airlines

There are airlines that disappear because they fail. Putin Princess Airlines disappeared because it became impossible to ignore.

THE RECORD

For a short period in ATC24, a fictional airline based in Kazakhstan operated on a doctrine no serious carrier would have approved and no one on frequency could forget. Its aircraft were military transports declared civilian by force of confidence. Its chief executive answered to the name Borat. Its radio calls were delivered in character. And for a while, against all reason, it worked.

I
THE FOUNDING

A flag carrier by declaration.

Putin Princess Airlines did not begin with investors, a route study, or a sensible fleet plan. It began with a claim: the C-17 Globemaster III was now a civilian airliner, Kazakhstan had a new carrier, and Borat was its CEO.

That was enough. Heavy transports began appearing at civilian airports under the Putin Princess name, flown not as military aircraft but as though they belonged to an established international airline. The fiction was never treated as a limitation. It was treated as company policy.

II
THE VOICE ON FREQUENCY

Every transmission became part of the brand.

Borat did not merely run the airline. He was its public address system, press department, captain, and permanent presence on frequency. Every flight was conducted with a Borat impression and an intentionally fractured command of aviation English.

“Putin Princess Airlines, can I get a yellow taxi to take me to the runway?”

Requests that should have been routine became company folklore. Controllers learned quickly that an ordinary taxi clearance might turn into a conversation no operations manual had prepared them for.

III
THE GOLDEN AGE

The joke acquired an airline-sized following.

Passengers and controllers began recognizing the callsign. People asked for smooth landings. PPA management answered with the confidence expected of an airline that had never once been constrained by reality.

“I will make landing as smooth as sandpaper. My passengers did not pay for safety.”

Ground controllers who cleared PPA IFR were rewarded with promotional discount codes for future flights. The discount code was, with impeccable corporate efficiency, simply the aircraft’s assigned squawk code.

What began as one pilot's recurring bit grew into a server of roughly forty people. Putin Princess had stopped being a private joke. It had become an institution.

IV
THE FALL

Then the callsign itself was grounded.

Most fictional airlines disappear quietly. Putin Princess Airlines achieved the rarer ending: it became popular enough that moderation intervened at the level of the name itself.

The callsign Putin Princess Airlines was prohibited. Using it could earn a server strike. There would be no restructuring, no rebrand presented as a strategic realignment, and no triumphant return under a holding company. The one thing that made the airline unmistakable had become the one thing it could no longer use.

Operations ended.

THE LAST FLIGHT

No farewell frequency was reserved.

There was no final revenue service. No retirement ceremony. No line of C-17s taxiing beneath an arch of water. One day the callsign belonged to the frequency; after that, it belonged only to memory.

The company server remained as proof that, for a moment, forty people had gathered around an airline whose safety policy was questionable, whose discount department issued transponder codes, and whose chief executive could turn a routine ground call into an event.

The aircraft were imaginary. The airline was a joke. The legend was real.
